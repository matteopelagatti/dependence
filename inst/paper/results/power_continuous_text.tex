We compared the test with eleven competitors on thirteen continuous dependence
schemes, each indexed by a noise standard deviation ranging from $0{\cdot}1$ to
$2{\cdot}0$ in steps of $0{\cdot}1$, at $n = 50$, $100$ and $200$, with
orthogonal polynomials and $10^4$ replications per configuration. Ten of the
schemes are those of the earlier literature \citep{New09, Hel13, Sim14, Rud17};
three are added here. The \textit{lattice} is a balanced $4\times4$ pattern with
exactly uniform margins in which every rank correlation vanishes identically, so
that dependence is visible only once the unit square is resolved;
\textit{sine 8pi} quadruples the frequency of the sine scheme, and is included
because a low-order smooth span is blind to high-frequency departures by
construction and the design should contain the case the method is worst at; and
\textit{Clayton} supplies the lower-tail dependence that none of the ten
possessed. All critical values are size-adjusted, taken from $2\times10^4$ null
datasets per $(n,p,q)$ for the statistics that depend on the data only through
the ranks, and from $5\times10^3$ permuted datasets per configuration for
distance covariance and HHG, which do not.

Two versions of our test appear. $B_n$ uses the single order of rule~(12),
giving $p = q = 2, 3, 4$. The \textit{order scan} of \S\ref{sec:scan} instead
computes the statistic at every order $k = 1,\ldots,K(n)$, converts each to its
exact null $p$-value and reports the smallest, rejecting when that minimum falls
below a threshold calibrated on the joint null of the whole family; $K$ was fixed
in advance at two orders above rule~(12), giving $K = 4, 5, 6$. The $K$
statistics are nested partial sums of one cross-moment array and are strongly
positively correlated, with null correlations between adjacent orders running
from $0{\cdot}41$ to $0{\cdot}81$ at $n = 100$, so calibrating on the joint null
rather than by Bonferroni recovers a factor of about $1{\cdot}6$ in the
threshold; that margin is what the scan converts into power.

Table~\ref{tab:power_summary} reports the mean over the $260$ configurations at
each sample size and Fig.~\ref{fig:power} the full profiles. The scan has the
highest mean power at all three sample sizes, $0{\cdot}467$, $0{\cdot}641$ and
$0{\cdot}790$, against $0{\cdot}434$, $0{\cdot}612$ and $0{\cdot}769$ for HHG
and $0{\cdot}429$, $0{\cdot}631$ and $0{\cdot}768$ for its atoms-based variant.
Its margin over the fixed-order test grows with the sample size, by
$0{\cdot}005$, $0{\cdot}043$ and $0{\cdot}075$, and the direction of that growth
is the point: rule~(12) lets the span grow only as $n^{0{\cdot}31}$, so the
restriction it imposes binds harder as $n$ increases, and a test that is not
obliged to commit to one order in advance escapes it. The ordering among the
remaining competitors is stable, with distance covariance, Hoeffding's statistic
and the Cram\'er--von Mises statistic of \citet{Gen19} within $0{\cdot}03$ of one
another at $n = 200$, and BET, the maximal information coefficient and
Chatterjee's $\xi$ trailing by a margin that widens with $n$.

No single competitor is ahead of the scan on balance. Counting the thirty-nine
scheme-by-sample-size combinations, distance covariance leads on seventeen of
them, Hoeffding's statistic and \citet{Gen19} on sixteen each, the atoms-based
HHG on eleven and HHG itself on seven; but each leads on a different part of the
design, and none has a competitive average. A per-scheme comparison against the
best competitor is therefore a comparison against an oracle that changes
identity from row to row, not against any procedure a practitioner could run.

What the averages conceal is that the tests are not competing on a common axis,
and the scheme-by-scheme profile is the more informative comparison. The scan is
the most powerful test on every scheme in which the dependence is neither
monotone nor of high frequency. At $n = 100$ it attains $0{\cdot}828$ on the
X-shape against $0{\cdot}780$ for the nearest competitor, $0{\cdot}811$ on the
lattice against $0{\cdot}792$, and $0{\cdot}567$ on the circle against
$0{\cdot}540$; on the lattice, distance covariance, Hoeffding's statistic and
\citet{Gen19} reach $0{\cdot}27$ or less, being close to powerless there by
construction, since the lattice has no rank correlation to detect.

The cases in which the test trails are equally systematic, and
Proposition~\ref{prop:lp_fixed} accounts for both exactly. The first is monotone
dependence: on the linear, Clayton, fourth-root and two-curves schemes the
departure is essentially one-dimensional, so the whole noncentrality sits in the
single coefficient $\theta_{11}$ while a fixed order spends $pq$ degrees of
freedom and pays a critical value of order $pq$ for them. Here the scan recovers
most of what the fixed order loses, since $k = 1$ is one of the orders it looks
at: on the linear scheme at $n = 100$ it reaches $0{\cdot}624$ against
$0{\cdot}516$ for rule~(12), and on two curves $0{\cdot}686$ against
$0{\cdot}584$, leaving distance covariance ahead at $0{\cdot}695$ and
$0{\cdot}756$ respectively. What remains of the gap is the price of not knowing
in advance that the relation is monotone, and the scan more than halves it. The
second case is high frequency: on the sine schemes the direction of departure
lies largely outside the span, the captured energy $\|\Pi_{pq}g\|^2$ is small,
and the noncentrality with it. On \textit{sine 8pi} at $n = 100$ both versions
reach about $0{\cdot}15$ against $0{\cdot}385$ for Chatterjee's $\xi$. Scanning
does not help here, and should not be expected to: every order it examines lies
in the same polynomial span, and the span is what fails. Raising $K$, or
changing the basis, is the instrument; that trade-off is what
\S\ref{sec:localpower} describes.

Scanning is not free, and the design shows where it is paid for. Where rule~(12)
already selects a well-matched order the scan gives a little back, on the cubic
scheme $0{\cdot}959$ against $0{\cdot}978$ at $n = 100$, and the fixed order
leads on eight of the thirty-nine combinations. The loss is bounded by the
calibration and is largest where the fixed-order test was already near one.

Finally, of the twelve statistics only $B_n$ and $P_n$ carry a reference
distribution in closed form, and the scan needs nothing more than a table of the
joint null simulated once per $(n, K)$ and reused for every dataset of that
size; it is still exactly distribution-free, because the whole family is a
function of the two rank vectors alone. Distance covariance and HHG are not
distribution-free at all and were calibrated here by permutation within every
configuration, a cost borne by this study rather than by the tests as reported;
in an application that calibration is the user's to pay, and
\S\ref{sec:computation} shows what it amounts to. At $n = 10^4$ a single
evaluation of the HHG statistic takes $98$ seconds against $0{\cdot}010$ for
$B_n$, and the statistic of \citet{Gen19} takes $3{,}491$ seconds, before any
permutation at all; at $n = 10^6$, where distance covariance and HHG cannot be
computed, $B_n$ takes $8{\cdot}7$ seconds and the scan $K$ times that. The
comparison on power is close; the comparison on what it costs to obtain a
$p$-value is not.
